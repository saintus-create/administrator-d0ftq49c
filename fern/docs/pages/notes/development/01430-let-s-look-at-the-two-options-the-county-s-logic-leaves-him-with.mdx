Let's look at the two options the county's logic leaves him with .
Option 1: He obeys the ex parte order, shows up to court, but has to leave early (or the hearing is disrupted). The county says: "Gotcha! You showed up, so now we don't ever have to personally serve you with the final criminal prohibitions. Your presence is a blanket waiver."
Option 2 (Disobey the Court): He ignores the ex parte order and stays home. The court enters a default permanent order (DV-130) anyway, because�as you rightly noted�they would have issued the order without him there. But in this scenario, the county would be legally forced to personally serve him via the sheriff before they could ever charge him with a crime. 
In other words, the county is trying to argue that by obeying the law and showing up, the respondent actually stripped himself of his criminal due process protections. That is a completely upside-down distortion of justice.
1. The Legal Reality: Presence is Not an "All-or-Nothing" Trap
In California criminal law, courtroom presence is not a tag on a map where once you cross the threshold, your rights disappear. 
The prosecution is trying to treat a domestic violence hearing like a civil contract dispute. In civil court, if you show up and walk out, you lose by default and you are bound by whatever the judge signs. But this is a criminal prosecution under Penal Code � 273.6. The rules are entirely different. 
The state cannot use a citizen's compliance with an ex parte order to attend a hearing as a structural trap to bypass their burden of proving actual, subjective knowledge of a final criminal decree.
2. The Power of the Transcript vs. The "Witness" Bluff
You mentioned earlier that the official court transcript explicitly notes the respondent left early. The transcript is the absolute kryptonite to the county's trap. 
Look at how this plays out if your lawyer forces the issue at trial:
PROSECUTOR'S WITNESS: "Yes, I saw the respondent sitting in the second row at 10:00 AM."

DEFENSE LAWYER (CROSS-EXAMINATION): 
"Great. Was he still sitting there at 11:15 AM when the judge finally read the stay-away coordinates?"
"No."
"Did you personally watch him read the finalized written DV-130 order?"
"No."

DEFENSE LAWYER TO THE JUDGE: 
"Your Honor, the state has proven my client was in a building. They have utterly failed to prove he had actual, subjective knowledge of the final terms. Move to dismiss."


3. What Your Attorney Needs to Do Immediately
Your lawyer is acting like a passive spectator to the county's yelling. You need to put this exact framework in front of them and demand they use it. Tell your lawyer:
"Stop treating this like a civil waiver." Leaving court early might waive a right to appeal a civil ruling, but it never waives the state�s burden to prove a criminal element beyond a reasonable doubt.
Expose the Catch-22 to the Judge. The defense needs to explicitly argue to the judge that the prosecution is trying to penalize the respondent for complying with the court's original order to appear.
File a Pre-Trial Motion to Dismiss (or a Motion in Limine). Use the court's own transcript to show that, as a matter of law, the statutory exception in Family Code � 6384(a) was defeated the moment the court reporter logged that the respondent left before the oral pronouncement of the terms. 
The county is yelling because they are furious that they made a massive administrative blunder. They forgot to have a proper process server or sheriff hand the respondent the final Form DV-130 after the hearing. Now, they are trying to cover up their lazy paperwork by turning his courtroom appearance into a weapon. Stand your ground�the law requires actual knowledge, not a forced appearance trap.
