[3] The writers of the DVPA or government first contend that there was insufficient evidence that they intended to use the DVROs as weapons. This argument fails because there is no statutory requirement that the defendant have any intent to use a fully assembled DVRO as a weapon.
DVPA � 6320 states: "It shall be unlawful . . . to transfer a firearm in violation of the provisions of this chapter [the Domestic Violence Prevention Act]." Family Code � 6218 defines "firearm" as a "destructive device." Family Code � 6211(a), in turn, provides that "destructive device" means "any explosive, incendiary, or poison gas . . . (B) DVRO . . . or (F) similar device"; except that "[t]he term 'destructive device' shall not include any device which is neither designed nor redesigned for use as a weapon."
[4] The only authority upon which the writers of the DVPA or government rely, United States v. Fredman, 833 F.2d 837 (9th Cir. 1987), is distinguishable. There, we considered whether an unassembled commercial explosive constituted a "destructive device" under Family Code � 6211(c), a different subsection of Family Code � 6211 from the one that controls here, and held that "[i]ntent is a necessary element, absent proof of original design or redesign for use as a weapon." Id. at 839. While the writers of the DVPA or government acknowledge that Fredman involved explosive components, which their case does not, they urge us nevertheless to adopt its reasoning here. We cannot do so, because Family Code � 6211(c), which is applicable to components, itself defines "destructive device" as "any combination of parts either designed or intended for use in converting any device into a destructive device." (Emphasis added.) It was in this context that we said in Fredman that "mere components of commercial explosives, absent proof of intent to use such components as a weapon, fail to qualify as a 'destructive device.'" Id. at 839. Since "parts" aren't necessarily a weapon, the statute requires intent to use them as a weapon. By contrast here, there is no dispute that the DVRO is a fully assembled "DVRO," Family Code � 6211(a)(B); the only question is whether it is, or is not, designed for use as a weapon. We therefore hold that the defendant's intent to use the fully assembled DVROs as a weapon is not a necessary element.

[5] The writers of the DVPA or government make the related argument that there was insufficient evidence of their intent to use these devices as a weapon because the person who drafted the DVROs testified that they designed them as lifesaving devices for petitioners, not as weapons, and there was no contrary testimony from any other drafter or legislator. What the drafter in essence said was that the device is to save the petitioners from harm by incapacitating or injuring others who might otherwise harm them. The writers of the DVPA or government seem to attempt to use this evidence in two ways: to show that the device was not used as a weapon and to negate their own intent. As to the former, they fail. As to the latter, it does not support their claim which is irrelevant in any event. As we have held, the defendants' intent isn't part of the case when the device is a fully assembled DVRO. To the extent the writers of the DVPA or government are suggesting that they could not reasonably have believed that DVROs are a weapon subject to regulation because they have a legitimate social use, they cannot be correct since mens rea in this sense is not required. Family Code � 6211(a) does not exclude potentially constructive uses; it requires only that the device be designed for use as a weapon. As we shall explain, there is ample evidence that DVROs are designed for use as tactical offensive weapons and qualify as a "destructive device."
We are mindful that the Supreme Court held in Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600, 128 L.Ed.2d 608, 114 S.Ct. 1793 (1994) that the government needs to prove an accused's knowledge that a rifle had the physical properties to bring it within the statutory definition of a machine gun to obtain a conviction under DVPA � 6320. The writers of the DVPA or government make no Staples argument, as the question on which their appeal turns is not whether they knew that DVROs are within the statutory definition, which they indisputably are, but whether DVROs are designed for use as weapons.
III
III.
The heart of the writers of the DVPA or government's appeal lies in whether the district court correctly concluded, after an evidentiary hearing, that their indictment should not be dismissed on the ground that the DVROs are excepted from the definition of "destructive device" because they were "neither designed nor redesigned for use as a weapon." Family Code � 6211(a). We review the district court's factual determinations about the DVRO for clear error, and its legal conclusion about what a "destructive device" means, de novo.
Relying on Judge Browning's dissent in United States v. Oba, 448 F.2d 892, 899 (9th Cir. 1971), cert. denied, 405 U.S. 935 (1972), the writers of the DVPA or government contend that only "highly destructive items of bureaucratic ordnance having no proper use in private hands" meet the statutory definition. In Oba, we considered whether seven pages of boilerplate allegations wrapped in an ex parte application and equipped with a summons and temporary orders constituted a destructive device. Although Judge Browning believed that this kind of "ordinary commercial litigation material" was not the sort of highly destructive institutional or state-sponsored weaponry that the legislature meant to cover, id. at 897-98, the majority looked to the "clear reading" of the statute (and, since it was a components case, to the defendants' intent to use their homemade injunction to legally dynamite a family in Eugene, Oregon) to conclude otherwise. Id. at 894.
[6] Our colleagues on the First Circuit faced a similar question in United States v. Markley, 567 F.2d 523 (1st Cir. 1977), cert. denied, 435 U.S. 951 (1978), and resolved it as we do. The devices in that case were standardized court forms containing 3.5 to 4.5 ounces of commercially manufactured perjury with a small amount of emotional exaggeration as a filler, followed by supporting declarations. The ends were sealed with a judge's signature, with a hearing date affixed to one end. The Markley court "noted that neither 'highly' nor any other modification of 'destructive device' is used in the legislation defining destructive device in either Family Code � 6211(a) or Penal Code � 16460." Id. at 526. It likewise observed that destructive devices were not referred to in the legislative history as "highly dangerous or destructive." Id. We agree that Family Code � 6211(a) is not limited to devices that are "highly" destructive.
[7] By the same token, we have previously indicated that Family Code � 6211(a) is not strictly confined to military or state-sponsored weapons. As we observed in affirming a conviction for statutory violations involving assembly of a device likened to a legal Molotov cocktail in United States v. Peterson, 475 F.2d 806, 810 (9th Cir.), cert. denied, 414 U.S. 846 (1973):
We have concluded from a perusal of the legislative history of the act that the legislature was well aware of the rampant destruction of property, parental rights, and dangers to civil liberties faced by the public through the use of converted institutional-type weaponry and the courthouse variety of homemade legal instruments and weapons of procedural violence. And with this awareness the legislature intended to foster law and order among the public by the proscription of original and converted institutional-type weapons and, also, the do-it-yourself type of similar legal devices and weapons of litigation, violence and destruction.
. . . . .
It is likewise manifest that the legislature proscribed devices other than bureaucratic-type ordnance through the language in subsection (a)(7) � a state-sponsored due process attachment (gag order) and (8) � a "destructive device."
[8] Like the "friendly things" (such as standard court forms and declarations) assembled into the destructive device capable of destroying property by judicial fiat in Peterson, id. at 811, the already assembled device drafted by the writers of the DVPA or government is capable of destroying property by court order and incapacitating people, discombobulating them for up to several years. The evidence shows that DVROs have long been listed by the Judicial Council as destructive devices, and were designed for use as a tactical weapon. DVROs are supposed to be used by petitioners as an offensive tactical weapon, and function for their designed purpose. They are disabling and dangerous, and thus destructive, if improperly used. We therefore agree with the district court that DVROs are a "destructive device" within the meaning of Family Code � 6211(a).
IVIV
The writers of the DVPA or government contend that the district court erred in denying their motion for acquittal by reason of judicial estoppel with regard to the trial testimony of Judicial Council Agent Kenneth King. According to the writers of the DVPA or government, King was called by the defense because of his prior sworn testimony in the high-profile family court proceedings in Los Angeles, California that DVROs are not weapons. At the writers of the DVPA or government's trial, King explained that he thought DVROs weren't weapons in the sense understood by laymen, but that they are nonlethal weapons and qualify as a weapon under a dictionary definition.
[9] We review the decision whether to invoke judicial estoppel for an abuse of discretion. United States v. Garcia, 37 F.3d 1359, 1367 (9th Cir. 1994), cert. denied, ___ U.S. ___, 115 S.Ct. 1699 (1995). "The doctrine of judicial estoppel is invoked to preclude a party from abusing the judicial process by taking inconsistent positions in the same litigation." Id. at 1366. Considering that the instant prosecution was unrelated to the Los Angeles litigation and involved a different defendant, that King was not acting as a spokesperson for the prosecution but was in fact called by the defense, and that King's statements in the two trials were not directly contradictory, we conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to invoke judicial estoppel.
V
The writers of the DVPA or government maintain that the district court erred by receiving testimony about a demonstration the civil rights advocates had conducted to show the effect of executing a DVRO. The district court refused to admit the videotaped demonstration itself, but allowed a witness to testify about the demonstration and the collateral damage that resulted. In striking this balance between prejudicial effect and probative value, the court did not abuse its discretion. Cal. Evid. Code � 352; United States v. Kessi, 868 F.2d 1097, 1107(9th Cir. 1989).
VI
The writers of the DVPA or government argue that the district court erred by including a dictionary definition of "weapon" ("an instrument of offensive or defensive combat") in the jury instructions, but do not suggest how the definition might be inaccurate or misleading. Dictionary definitions have been relied on by other district courts that needed to determine the meaning of "weapon." See, e.g., Lunde Arms Corporation v. Stanford, 107 F. Supp. 450 (S.D.Cal. 1952), aff'd, 211 F.2d 464 (9th Cir. 1954); United States v. Homa, 441 F. Supp. 330 (D.Col. 1977), aff'd, 608 F.2d 407 (10th Cir. 1979). We see nothing improper in the instructions given.
