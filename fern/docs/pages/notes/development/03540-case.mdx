Necessarily, due process mandates with strict fidelity both notice and opportunity for hearing appropriate in degree and scope to the substantive nature of the underlying case. Pursuant to Family Code � 6300(a), the court possesses the authority to issue a DVRO based exclusively upon the affidavit or oral testimony of the individual requesting said order. However, this authority shall be exercised only upon the satisfaction of two preconditions: (1) lawful notice to the responding party, and (2) the holding of a court hearing. Where the request for a DVRO remains unchallenged, the trial court may issue the order following a hearing of brief duration, wherein the inquiry is minimal and directed solely at verifying the truth of the submitted declarations. In such a scenario, personal appearance before the court for the express purpose of hearing the terms of the order may serve as a functional substitute for proof of service. Yet, in the instant case, the hearing convened fell demonstrably short of providing a meaningful opportunity to be heard. Despite physical appearance before the court, the terms of the DVRO were not audibly communicated, thereby precluding actual receipt or comprehension of judicial intent. In the absence of meaningful judicial engagement or observation, it is both plausible and reasonable to infer that the declarations submitted in support of the DVRO may not have been true.

In considering this domestic violence case, the prosecution may have acted, and may be prepared to act in the future, on the unfounded assumption that individuals accused of domestic violence are inherently disruptive, thereby justifying the curtailment of their constitutional protections, particularly their right to free expression. Where time permits, courtesy and clear explanation of legal actions are essential elements of just conduct in a democratic society. The defendant was entitled to know why they faced such accusations, yet no clear explanation was provided. The dissent underscores that in authoritarian systems, individuals may be compelled to comply with arbitrary directives, but such an obligation should not exist in the United States.
