III. LEGAL STANDARD: SELECTIVE AND DISCRIMINATORY PROSECUTION
The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and the California Constitution (Art. I, � 7) stand as bulwarks against discriminatory enforcement of the laws. Wayte v. United States, 470 U.S. 598, 608 (1985); Murgia v. Municipal Court, 15 Cal. 3d 286, 296-301 (1975).
While prosecutorial discretion is broad, it is explicitly limited by constitutional commands against arbitrary or unjustifiable classifications. United States v. Armstrong, 517 U.S. 456, 464 (1996); People v. Superior Court (Hartway), 19 Cal. 3d 338, 349 (1977).
To establish a prima facie case of selective prosecution, a defendant must demonstrate both key elements:
(a) That they were deliberately singled out for prosecution while others similarly situated were not; AND
(b) That this discriminatory selection was based on an impermissible motive like race, religion, gender, or retaliation for exercising constitutional rights. (Parallel Syntax emphasizing the two required prongs)Armstrong, 517 U.S. at 465; Murgia, 15 Cal. 3d at 300.
This requires showing "intentional and purposeful discrimination." Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944); Baluyut v. Superior Court, 12 Cal. 4th 826, 832 (1996).
Upon such a prima facie showing, the burden shifts to the prosecution to prove legitimate, non-discriminatory grounds for the prosecution. United States v. Berrios, 501 F.2d 1207, 1211 (2d Cir. 1974); People v. Smith, 155 Cal. App. 3d 1103, 1133 (1984).
