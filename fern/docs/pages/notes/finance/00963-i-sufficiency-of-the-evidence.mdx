I. Sufficiency of the Evidence
M.C. challenges the trial court�s issuance of a domestic violence restraining order (DVRO) under the Domestic Violence Prevention Act (DVPA), asserting insufficient evidence, due process violations, and unconstitutional restrictions on her protected speech. We conclude that the DVRO lacks substantial evidence, is procedurally defective, and infringes M.C.�s First Amendment rights. A.Q., a martyr/dominator abuser, manipulates M.C.�s attachment needs through a pattern of dominance, initiated by a unilateral, undisclosed 2018 breakup and extended through collusionary, hypothetical, and boilerplate alarmist allegations rooted in patriarchal stock stories. Leveraging parallel construction and misleading half-truths, A.Q. silences M.C.�s credibility, weaponizing her post-abuse victim behavior to legally and socially imprison her. Procedural failures and systemic complicity in gender-based violence invalidate the order.
A. Standard of Review
The trial court exercises broad discretion under the DVPA to grant a DVRO. (M.S. v. A.S. (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 1139, 1143.) We review for abuse of discretion, applying the substantial evidence rule to factual findings, accepting evidence supporting the judgment and resolving conflicts in its favor. (Ibid.) Absent a statement of decision, we infer all necessary findings, upholding those supported by substantial evidence and reviewing the conclusion for abuse of discretion. (Fair v. Bakhtiari (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1135, 1148.) The application of the correct legal standard is reviewed de novo. (Ashby v. Ashby (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 491, 509.) M.C. argues the trial court misapplied the DVPA�s �disturbing the peace� standard, violated due process, and improperly restricted speech, necessitating de novo review of legal and procedural errors.
B. The DVPA
The DVPA authorizes a DVRO to prevent domestic violence recurrence upon reasonable proof of past abuse. (� 6300; Nevarez v. Tonna (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 774, 782.) Abuse includes conduct enjoinable under section 6320, such as harassing or disturbing the peace, defined as destroying mental or emotional calm, including coercive control that interferes with free will or liberty. (� 6203, subds. (a)(4), (b); � 6320, subds. (a), (c); In re Marriage of Nadkarni (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 1483, 1497.) While section 6300 permits issuance based solely on the petitioner�s affidavit, this discretion requires procedural safeguards and evidentiary rigor. We grant A.Q.�s motion for judicial notice of legislative materials and take judicial notice of additional relevant materials. (In re J.W. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 200, 211.)
C. Narrative and Analysis
A.Q., a martyr/dominator abuser, initiated his control over M.C. through a unilateral, undisclosed 2018 breakup, denying her agency and exploiting her attachment needs�her capacity for love and concern. As a dominator, A.Q. orchestrated this betrayal to assert power; as a martyr, he later feigned victimhood, framing M.C.�s expressions of care as harassment to criminalize her vulnerability. This manipulation, the �cruelest lie,� instills fear that M.C.�s care could lead to legal or social consequences, perpetuating a patriarchal stock story that upholds male dominance by subordinating female survivors. (See Delgado, Storytelling for Oppositionists and Others: A Plea for Narrative (1989) 87 Mich. L. Rev. 2411, 2414.) M.C.�s text messages accusing A.Q. of abuse and 2021 Facebook posts tagging his associates are post-abuse victim behavior, protected illocutionary acts�assertions and criticisms�triggered by A.Q.�s emotional abuse and betrayal. (Rodriguez v. Menjivar (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 816, 822.) These acts, lacking intent to harass, aim to reclaim agency and warn others, not to cause harm.
A.Q.�s DVRO petition extends his dominance, leveraging collusionary, hypothetical, and boilerplate alarmist allegations to silence M.C.�s credibility and legally imprison her. His allegations align with stock stories that cast men as victims of irrational women, dismissing M.C.�s trauma as harassment. The collusionary nature suggests coordination, possibly with professional contacts, to amplify his martyr narrative. The hypothetical quality manifests in vague claims (e.g., �300 texts�) lacking specific evidence. The boilerplate alarmist tone employs fear-inducing rhetoric, omitting A.Q.�s manipulative breakup, constituting misleading half-truths. (Randi W. v. Muroc Joint Unified Sch. Dist. (1997) 14 Cal.4th 1066, 1082.) The �300 texts� claim likely reflects parallel construction, sanitizing illicitly obtained evidence to support A.Q.�s narrative. (Levine et al., Evidence Laundering in a Post-Herring World, 106 J. Crim. L. & Criminology 627, 663 (2016).) This testimonial injustice�attributing an unfair credibility deficit to M.C.�silences her voice, preventing her assertions from registering as valid. (Fricker, Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing (2007) 140�141.)
The trial court�s findings�that M.C.�s posts caused A.Q.�s CPTSD attack, eviction, and isolation�lack substantial evidence. A.Q.�s distress, a perlocutionary effect, is strategically amplified to justify the DVRO, exploiting the DVPA�s affidavit-based process (� 6300). Perlocutionary effects, unlike illocutionary acts, lack inherent protection, and M.C.�s posts, as avoidable speech, do not justify restrictions. (Erznoznik v. City of Jacksonville (1975) 422 U.S. 205, 210�211.) A.Q.�s distress could stem from pre-existing conditions or professional setbacks, not M.C.�s actions, undermining causation. The DVRO constitutes false imprisonment, restricting M.C.�s speech, social interactions, and professional engagement, akin to unlawful confinement. (People v. Agnew (1940) 16 Cal.2d 655, 659.) This imprisonment perpetuates A.Q.�s dominance, chilling M.C.�s First Amendment rights through an overbroad prior restraint. (Balboa Island Village Inn, Inc. v. Lemen (2007) 40 Cal.4th 1141, 1155.)
Procedural irregularities exacerbate the DVRO�s invalidity. The trial court failed to provide Form FL-320, required under Code of Civil Procedure, � 1005, California Rules of Court, rule 5.92, and the Fourteenth Amendment, denying M.C. notice and opportunity to contest A.Q.�s claims. This due process violation, combined with reliance on A.Q.�s misleading affidavit, enables his stock story of male victimhood, perpetuating systemic gender-based violence. The DVPA�s affidavit-based process (� 6300) facilitates abuser manipulation, silencing survivors like M.C. through testimonial injustice. (In re Marriage of Davila & Mejia (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 220, 226.)
M.C.�s posts, protected illocutionary acts, lack the intent to harass required for DVPA abuse. (Counterman v. Colorado (2023) 600 U.S. 66, 74; State v. Hoffman (N.J. 1997) 149 N.J. 564, 577.) Restrictions on speech require narrow tailoring to a significant governmental interest, leaving ample alternative channels. (Ward v. Rock Against Racism (1989) 491 U.S. 781, 791.) The DVRO, targeting M.C.�s content without evidence of unavoidable exposure, fails this standard. Unlike cases involving invasive conduct (Altafulla v. Ervin (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 571), M.C.�s actions resemble interpersonal disputes, not domestic violence. (Curcio v. Pels (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 1, 13.) The DVPA is not a forum for reputational disputes, which belong in defamation law.
The trial court�s failure to scrutinize A.Q.�s manipulation of M.C.�s attachment needs, his stock stories, or her victim status, compounded by procedural and systemic failures, renders the DVRO invalid. Subjective distress alone does not justify a DVRO; objective abuse is required. (� 6320, subd. (c).) The order, with criminal penalties and social stigma, demands rigorous evidentiary scrutiny, absent here. (Curcio, supra, 47 Cal.App.5th at p. 13, fn. 6.) Under de novo review, the court erred in restricting protected speech and equating reputational harm with abuse, perpetuating state-complicit oppression.
