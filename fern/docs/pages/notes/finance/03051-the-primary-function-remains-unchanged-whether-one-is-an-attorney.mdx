The primary function remains unchanged: whether one is an attorney or not is inconsequential. What is paramount is that making accusations based on emotions, speculation, or convenience�particularly at the expense of another�is unacceptable. An accusation, if it is to possess any legitimacy, must be grounded in verifiable facts. In this instance, the claims made against me lack all such determination: there are no names, no times, no places, not even consistent or accurate pronouns. There is no evidence that he was aware, in fact, of the reasons for which I was contacting him.

From this absence of concrete determination, he nevertheless drew a definitive conclusion�that I was being abusive. This conclusion could not have been reached through verification, as no such verification occurred. I provided him with no prior cause, no established pattern, no articulated act that could reasonably be examined as abuse. Instead, assumptions were formed in isolation, mediated and interpreted by third parties, and then returned to me as judgment.

He further claimed that I was manipulative. However, rather than confronting me directly�rather than engaging in communication, which is the fundamental aspect of any human relationship�he chose to complain to others. In doing so, he denied me both the opportunity to comprehend how my actions were perceived and the chance to rectify them. �Manipulation� is a broad and ideologically charged term. All social interactions involve influence; even a teacher �manipulates� conditions so that learning may occur. I am not minimizing harm, but I insist that categories must be specified, not weaponized.

If every couple, instead of communicating, were to externalize their conflicts�reporting one another to third parties whenever discomfort arose�then all relationships would dissolve into surveillance and rumor rather than mutual recognition.

Compounding this issue is his established pattern of abandonment: disappearing without notice, withdrawing communication, leaving me to speculate whether he was serious or merely �in a mood.� This time, I attempted to break that cycle materially and constructively. I proposed couples therapy. I engaged with literature on his self-identified �avoidant� personality type. I did so because, without some shared framework or confirmation, the dynamic degenerates into a one-sided arrangement: I am expected to remain faithful, stable, and available, while he retains freedom without accountability�keeping me bound while he acts without constraint.

To be clear, he occupies no more the position of a victim than did Southern slaveholders who imagined themselves persecuted by Northern abolitionist pressure. In both cases, what is experienced as harm is not injury but the destabilization of an asymmetrical arrangement. The language of victimhood here does not describe suffering; it functions as a defensive strategy deployed when unaccountable power is challenged.

This is not mutuality; it is domination disguised as ambiguity. Historically, such arrangements resemble those enforced by cult leaders, who compel their partners into coerced polyamory while reserving privilege for themselves. I was not willing to participate in that structure. If that was his intention, I would not consent to it.

Yet his refusal to reply placed me in a double bind. If I left the relationship, I would be cast as cruel for abandoning him while he was merely �needing space.� If I stayed silent, I would be coerced into compliance by his absence. This is not neutrality; it is power exercised through withdrawal.

What occurred over those months was not benign. It was not simply space. It was a condition produced by non-communication, unverified accusation, and asymmetrical control�conditions that no healthy relationship, and no just social relation, can sustain.
