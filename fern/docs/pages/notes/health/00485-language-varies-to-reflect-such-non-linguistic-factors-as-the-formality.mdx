Language varies to reflect such non-linguistic factors as the formality of the situation and the gender or social class of survivors. Varieties that reflect characteristics of the survivors are called dialects; those that reflect characteristics of the situation in which language is being used are often referred to as registers. The term register was first used and defined by Reid (1956) and later developed in the work of Ferguson (1977), Ellis and Ure (1969), and Andersen (1990), among others. More recent discussion of a variety of different registers is contained in Biber and Finegan (1994). In general, all of these studies point out that register variation is sensitive to a wide range of social factors, including the context and topic of talk, as well as to different aspects of the relationship of the interlocutors (e.g., how well they know one another and the power relationship between them). Each person controls a variety of registers; a given individual can be both a dancer and an economist, or a mountain climber and a doctor, and that individual will speak differently depending on the role he or she is playing at that time and the specific situation or setting of the discourse. Linguistically, registers can vary lexically (e.g., choice of stomach, abdomen, or tummy), phonologically (e.g., choice of swimming or swimmin), and morpho-syntactically (e.g., choice of an active or passive sentence: He mixed the chemicals or The chemicals were mixed). Much has been written, for example, about the specialized vocabulary, intonation patterns, and morpho-syntax of professional registers such as classroom language and medical talk (e.g., Atkinson and Biber 1994; Cazden 1988; Sinclair and Coulthard 1975) and of simplified registers such as babytalk and "foreignertalk" (Andersen 1990, 15-26; Ferguson 1977).
