Plaintiff alleges that the California Departments of Mental Health and Addiction Services, Social Services, and Health violate the ADA and the Rehabilitation Act. The actual reasons for termination are in dispute, but the stated reason was that Plaintiff was routinely denied services through providers for personal reasons unrelated to care. The State�s Medical Managed, County-Organized Health System, CenCal, implemented a protocol for Plaintiff to follow when these denials occurred. Plaintiff notified CenCal, DHCS, and DMHC that this protocol would not work because of his disabilities.

Ultimately, Plaintiff contacted a specialist and completed an intake. Although the specialist normally did not accept uninsured patients, they agreed to make an exception in Plaintiff�s case due to the severity of his needs. Plaintiff requested a modification to the protocol to allow him to use it. CenCal refused to implement the modification. The denials continued, and eventually Plaintiff was terminated from the provider�s program on these grounds for non-compliance. This abrupt termination cut him off from critical social and provider supports, causing him to become depressed, hospitalized, and victimized by others in the community who saw Plaintiff as an easy target, while placing him at risk of unnecessary institutionalization.

It is fundamentally unfair to demand that a psychiatric patient prove his own sanity or nondangerousness. Yet this is exactly what is imposed here. Plaintiff is required�repeatedly�to justify himself, to recount the same explanations to each new provider, even as the preceding providers� reasoning is amorphous, contradictory, or impossible to decipher. It is self-evident that a patient in this position is effectively helpless and has almost no meaningful chance of prevailing in such a system.

In practical terms, Plaintiff has been involuntarily committed to a revolving sequence of dismissing providers for mental-health treatment�an arrangement that, in all but name, mirrors the custodial elements of commitment. Where, as here, the governing statutes create specific procedural protections, and those mandated procedures are ignored, any form of involuntary confinement or compelled treatment relationship becomes improper.

The importance of this principle cannot be overstated. The county�s failure to follow required procedures leaves Plaintiff functionally detained within a closed loop of provider dismissals and reassignments, without any way to exit the system or even fully understand the reasons used to justify continued deprivation of appropriate care.




Plaintiffs are individuals with disabilities who receive Medi-Cal benefits to remain safely at home without risk of serious injury or harm. Plaintiffs seek to prevent the State from implementing reductions in Medicaid services through revised eligibility criteria that would reduce, terminate, or modify these benefits. Plaintiffs assert that the loss of services would expose them to a serious risk of unnecessary institutionalization, in violation of the Medicaid Act and the ADA�s integration mandate.

Federal law is clear that states must do more than simply authorize services�they have an affirmative obligation to ensure that eligible individuals actually receive them. When applying this principle, when a state fails to arrange and deliver necessary Medicaid-covered supports, it forces people with disabilities toward institutional settings and violates the requirement that services be provided in the �most integrated setting appropriate.� See Ivory N. v. Kent, Director of CA DHCS (N.D. Cal. May 24, 2018); Chambers et al. v. City and County of San Francisco (N.D. Cal. 2006). These decisions recognize that pushing the burden of locating or securing essential care onto disabled individuals causes predictable harms�medical deterioration, psychological injury, hospitalization, and displacement from the community�and constitutes discriminatory segregation. See also Doe v. Pasadena Unified School District (W.D. Cal. 2016).

Because Plaintiffs face these same risks, and because they have been determined capable of safely living in their homes and communities, Plaintiffs demand the following relief:

	1.	That the State be enjoined from reducing, terminating, or modifying the Medi-Cal services at issue through the proposed eligibility changes;
	2.	That the State be required to ensure the actual delivery�not merely the authorization�of all medically necessary services essential to keeping Plaintiffs safely in the community;
	3.	That the State implement effective systems for arranging staffing, medication access, and approved Medi-Cal benefits so that Plaintiffs are not forced into institutional settings;
	4.	That the State comply with the ADA and Section 504 by providing services in the most integrated setting appropriate to Plaintiffs� needs;
	5.	Any further relief necessary to prevent the imminent risk of unnecessary institutionalization and to preserve Plaintiffs� ability to remain stable, safe, and independent in the community.
