In Martin v. Martin, 822 S.E.2d 756 (N.C. Ct. App. 2018), the court held that the "admission of testimony of domestic violence not otherwise pleaded in a complaint and motion for a domestic violence protective order violates a defendant�s right to due process."
Similarly, in H.E.S. v. J.C.S., 793 A.2d 780 (N.J. Super. Ct. App. Div. 2002), the court found it a "fundamental violation of due process" to proceed on acts not alleged in the complaint because the "Defendant could not prepare a defense to charges that he was not even told about until the day of the hearing."
Under California law, due process requires that all parties be notified of the facts and issues in dispute and be afforded a fair opportunity to present evidence. As held in North Coast Village Condominium Assn. v. Phillips (Cal. Ct. App. 2023), it is a "fundamental concept of due process that a judgment against a defendant cannot be entered unless [they were] given proper notice and an opportunity to defend."

B. FAMILY CODE � 6384 - FAILURE TO "HEAR THE TERMS"

Even if the hearing were valid, the subsequent arrest is unlawful because the Respondent never received notice as required by California law. Family Code � 6384(a) creates an exception to the personal service requirement only if the respondent "has received actual notice of the existence and substance of the order through personal appearance in court to hear the terms of the order from the court."

Analysis of "to hear" requirement: The statute's plain language is conjunctive. It requires both (1) personal appearance and (2) that the appearance was for the purpose of and resulted in hearing the terms. Mere presence in the virtual courtroom at the start of a hearing is insufficient. The purpose of the appearance must be fulfilled.
Judicial Council Benchguide: This interpretation is confirmed by California's DVRO Benchguide, which notes that the absence of the criterion "heard the terms of the order from the court" is an affirmative defense to an enforcement action.
