*1 I. INTRODUCTION
Appellant, Michael Bowen, has filed his second appeal in this case. This time, Mr. Bowen challenges the renewal of a domestic violence restraining order against him. This appeal continues Mr. Bowen's extensive history of litigation abuse against Respondent, Sonya Lister. Appellant claims that the trial court improperly renewed Ms. Lister's restraining order, and that the order unconstitutionally burdens his associational rights. This appeal reinforces Ms. Lister's justification for the renewal. Mr. Bowen is a stalker who can't move on.
Respondent respectfully requests that the Court uphold the trial court's renewal of her domestic violence restraining order against Appellant. The trial court properly applied the �reasonable apprehension of future abuse� test, properly considered Appellant's excessive litigation when it found that there had not been a change in circumstances, and properly ordered Appellant to stay away from Ms. Lister's place of employment. Furthermore, Appellant's appeal is untimely as it was filed more than ninety days after he filed his motion to reconsider.
II. STATEMENT OF THE CASE
A. STATEMENT OF THE FACTS
This case involves a four week relationship that spawned, in Ms. Lister's own words, �four telephone books� worth of court files. (See Letter from Michael Bowen dated July 17, 2008 in Respondent's Request *2 for Order filed July 24, 2008 at Exhibit 1 to Respondent's Motion to Augment at 10 (hereafter �Augmented Record� or �AR�); RT 11-9-11 at 10:28-11:1.) Sonya Lister and Michael Bowen dated for a few weeks in 2008. (RT 9-10-08 at Exhibit 3 to Respondent's Motion to Augment at 6:2-3.) Ms. Lister ended the relationship due to Mr. Bowen's erratic behavior. (Emergency Protective Order at AR 11.) Mr. Bowen started to stalk Ms. Lister at work and home, prompting her to file for a domestic violence restraining order against him on July 24, 2008. (AR 1-11.) Since then, Appellant has continued his harassment through litigation. (RT 11-9-11 at 10:7-11:1.)

On the first occasion, Mr. Bowen �pop[ed] up� and banged on Ms. Lister's car. (Id. at 6.) He told her that he had seen her walking by and called her cell phone. (Ibid.) When she didn't answer, he decided to drive to the parking lot, because he knew to where she was walking. (Ibid.) When Ms. Lister told him he needed to move on from their relationship, he said that he loved her, and she told him to leave her alone. (Ibid.) On a *3 second occasion, on or about July 10, 2008, Mr. Bowen startled Ms. Lister in the parking lot again. (Id. at 5.) Ms. Lister reminded him that she asked him to leave her alone, and Appellant said that he loved her and indicated that he was stalking her. (Ibid.) Mr. Bowen told Ms. Lister that he had watched her in the parking lot before, and that he often watched her without her knowledge. (Ibid.)
Appellant continued to drive by her home, sometimes parking his car, walking up her driveway, and looking through her windows. (AR 7.) Ms. Lister saw Mr. Bowen's car three blocks from her home, which alarmed her given she lived in Hayward and he lived across the Bay in Daly City. (RT 9-10-08 at 4:1-7.)
Mr. Bowen intrusively tried to insinuate himself into Ms. Lister's family. He contacted Ms. Lister's teenage daughter without Ms. Lister's permission, and had the daughter come outside of their home so that he could speak to her. (AR 7; RT 9-10-08 at 4:24-26.) On July 16, 2008, Mr. Bowen approached Ms. Lister as she was walking down the street, even though she had repeatedly told him that she did not want to speak to him anymore. (AR 4.) After she told him to leave her alone and walked away, he called her and left her a voicemail. (Ibid.)
On July 17, 2008, an officer with the San Francisco Police Department took a statement from Ms. Lister and listened to the voice messages left on her phone by Mr. Bowen. (RT 11-9-11 at 36:17-18.) The officer assisted Ms. Lister in obtaining an Emergency Protective Order against Mr. Bowen. (AR 11; RT 11-9-11 at 36:18-21.) In violation of the emergency protective order, Mr. Bowen then mailed a letter to Ms. Lister's home. (AR 10-11; RT 11-9-11 at 36:21-23.)
At the September 10, 2008 hearing on Ms. Lister's retraining order request, the court found that Mr. Bowen was stalking Ms. Lister. (RT 9-10-08 at 18:4.) The court addressed Mr. Bowen directly about his behavior:

C. STANDARD OF REVIEW
 
The standard of review for a domestic violence restraining order is abuse of discretion. (Nakamura v. Parker (2007) 156 Cal.App.4th 327, 333, citing Quintana v. Guijosa (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 1077, 1079.) A trial court has not abused its discretion unless, in light of all circumstances and considerations, the court's decision �exceeds the bounds of reason.� (Denham v. Superior Court (1970) 2 Cal.3d 557, 566 [citation].) The complaining party carries the burden to establish an abuse of discretion. (Id.) The constitutional arguments raised by Appellant as to overbreadth of the order do require independent review. (Evans v. Evans (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 1157, 1166.)
*12 III. LEGAL DISCUSSION
A. THE COURT PROPERLY RENEWED THE RESTRAINING ORDER BASED ON RESPONDENT'S REASONABLE FEAR OF FUTURE ABUSE, WHICH WAS JUSTIFIED GIVEN APPELLANT'S VIOLATION OF THE ORDER AND HIS LITIGATION ABUSE
1. A Restraining Order May Be Renewed Based on an Objectively Reasonable Apprehension of Future Abuse
A court may renew a protective order under the Domestic Violence Prevention Act for five years or permanently. (Fam. Code, � 6345 subd. (a).)3 If the request to renew is contested, the court must find by a preponderance of the evidence that the protected party has a genuine and objectively �reasonable apprehension� of future abuse. (Ritchie v. Konrad (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 1275, 1290 (hereafter Ritchie).) The party seeking to renew need not show any further abuse since the issuance of the original order. (Fam. Code, � 6345, subd. (a).)
The leading case, Ritchie v. Konrad, lays out three factors for courts to consider when determining whether the protected party has a �reasonable apprehension� of future abuse. (Ritchie, supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at 1290-1292.) First, the trial court should take into account the findings and facts *13 underlying the initial protective order. (Id. at 1290.) Second, the trial court should consider whether any significant changes in circumstances since the issuance of the initial order are relevant. (Id. at 1291.) Finally, the burdens that the protective order creates for the restrained party are sometimes a relevant factor. (Ibid.)
If the protected party has a �reasonable apprehension� of future physical abuse, then the court should renew the order regardless of the burdens on the restrained party. (Ritchie, supra, 115 Cal.App.4th at 1292.) If the protected party has a �reasonable apprehension� of other forms of abuse, then the court shall weigh �the seriousness as well as the degree of the risk against the significance of the burdens.� (Ibid.)
The court concluded that Ms. Lister was genuinely apprehensive and her apprehension was reasonable under the Ritchie standard. (RT 11-9-11 at 70:21-23, 71:25-27.) The trial court properly assessed each of the three factors as described below. (Id. at 69:24-70:1, 71:13-17, 72:1-21.)
2. Ms. Lister's Fear of Future Abuse Was Reasonable Given Appellant's History of Stalking and His Restraining Order Violations
Mr. Bowen's preferred mode of abuse is stalking. (RT 9-10-08 at 18:2-5.) The first restraining order was based on Appellant's stalking of Ms. Lister after she ended their relationship. (Ibid.) Ms. Lister's restraining order renewal was based on several incidents which indicated Mr. Bowen continued to try to weave his web around her.
*14 Mr. Bowen chose to violate the restraining order by going to Ms. Lister's place of work just two months before the order was due to expire. (RT 11-9-11 at 39:20-24.) This incident caused Ms. Lister great distress, which was observed by her co-worker, Angela David. (Id. at 43:12-15.) Ms. Lister missed work due to the stress. (Id. at 44:10-16.) Ms. David noted: �It just like feels unsafe and that he'll be anywhere.� (Id. at 44:25-26.)
Mr. Bowen had other contact with Ms. Lister's employer that upset her. He came to one of her job sites after the initial restraining order was issued (RT 9-7-11 at 4:12-14), and sent a letter to Ms. Lister's employer about the restraining order. (RT 11-9-11 at 5:25-6:13.) He also contacted her employer to accuse her of paycheck fraud. (Id. at 11:27-12:3, 12:16-19.)
Mr. Bowen admitted that he continues to spend time in Hayward, where Ms. Lister lives, even though he lives in Daly City. (RT 11-09-11 at 67:14-17, 68:2-12.) Ms. Lister and a friend observed him just three blocks from Ms. Lister's residence. (Id. at 68:9-11; RT 9-7-11 at 7:13-19.) He also continued to divide Ms. Lister's family. (RT 11-9-11 at 11:6-16.) He tried to involve Ms. Lister's aunt in testifying against Ms. Lister. (Id. at 69:1-9.)
The court found Ms. Lister's fear of future abuse to be reasonable. (RT 11-9-11 at 70:21-26.) Particularly since the initial stalking occurred *15 near Ms. Lister's workplace and the restraining order violation occurred there as well, Ms. Lister's fear was reasonable. (Id. at 71:18-22, 72:27-73:3.) The court emphasized that it was �very concerning� that Appellant had knowingly violated the restraining order. (Id. at 71:5-8.) The court noted the number of attorneys Appellant had retained, which gave him ample access to legal advice in order to understand the restraining order. (Id. at 73:1-3.) Appellant's obsessiveness, so remarked upon by the court which issued the initial restraining order (RT 9-10-08 at 18:1-17; RT 2-5-09 10:18-11:13), was also observed by the different judicial officer who renewed the order. (RT 11-9-11 at 71:8-11.)
In addition to the incident at Ms. Lister's work, the trial court also noted that Ms. Lister's �concerns about Mr. Bowen following her, concerns about his involvement in her family in a way that wasn't appropriate are sufficient reasons for her to have an apprehension of future abuse.� (RT 11-09-11 at 70:23-26.)
Since no further abuse is necessary to renew a restraining order, renewal is entirely appropriate when the restrained party has actually violated the order. (See Avalos, supra, 196 Cal.App.4th at 775, 777 [when defendant's friend came to plaintiffs work a month before the restraining order was due to expire and told her that defendant sent his �Greetings,� this conduct was ample support for plaintiffs fear of defendant and the restraining order was properly renewed].)
*16 Appellant argues that Ms. Lister's fears, while genuine, are not reasonable. (Appellant's Opening Brief at 10 (hereafter AOB).) Reasonableness is a matter to be determined by the trier of fact. (People v. Furber (1965) 233 Cal.App.2d 678, 685 [in homicide case where the defendant killed her abusive ex-husband, it was inappropriate for the appellate court to determine whether the defendant's fears were reasonable, as this was a matter for the jury to determine].) The trial court in this case, who observed all of the witnesses, determined that Ms. Lister's fear of future abuse was reasonable. The appellate court must �accept as true all evidence... tending to establish the correctness of the trial court's findings [citation]. (Sabbah v. Sabbah (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 818, 823 [in a restraining order appeal where the restrained party challenged the factual findings of the trial court, the reviewing court found that it must resolve every conflict in evidence in favor of the trial court's decision].)

5. Ms. Lister Had No Motive to Fabricate Domestic Violence
Appellant alleges that Ms. Lister fabricated allegations of domestic violence in order to gain the benefits of the Violence Against Women Act, (Pub. L. 109-162, 119 Stat. 2960 (2006), (hereafter VAWA)) to protect her federally subsidized housing assistance. (AOB 13.) VAWA creates certain protections for abuse survivors in public housing. Abuse survivors cannot be denied housing or evicted because they are victims of domestic violence. (42 U.S.C. �� 1437d(c)(3), 1437d (1)(5)-(1)(6), 1437f(c)(9)(A)-(c)(9)(C), *20 1437f(d)(1)(A)-(d)(1)(C), 1437f(o)(6)(1), 1437f(o)(7)(C)o)(7)(D), 1437f(o)(20)(A)-(o)(20)(B).) Ms. Lister admitted that she had a criminal conviction related to her failure to report her full income to the housing authority seven years ago. (RT 11-9-11 at 41:21-22.) Mr. Bowen argues that this housing issue motivated her to lie about his stalking so that she could be covered by VAWA.
Appellant misstates the extent of the protections provided to domestic violence and stalking victims under VAWA. VAWA provides protections only when there is a connection between the domestic violence and the reason for the housing denial or the eviction. (42 U.S.C. �� 1437d(1)(6)(D), 1437f(o)(7)(D)(iv) and (o)(20)(D)(iii), 1437f(1)(9)(C)(iv) and (d)(1)(B)(iii)(IV).) Any criminal activity that would normally disqualify a tenant from receiving assistance must be �directly related� to the domestic violence or stalking in order for the victim to qualify for the exemption. (24 C.F.R. � 5.2005 (c)(2).)
In Ms. Lister'S case, there is no connection between Mr. Bowen's stalking and her underpayment to the Housing Authority. Ms. Lister's restraining order renewal in 2011 would in no way demonstrate that her inadequate income reporting years earlier was directly caused by a person she didn't date until 2008. The conviction, and any subsequent negative housing repercussions it might have, had no bearing on her motive for renewing her order, and thus evidence of it was not relevant to the case.
*21 B. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY CONSIDERED APPELLANT'S EXCESSIVE LITIGATION WHEN IT REISSUED THE PROTECTIVE ORDER, AS HIS LITIGATION WAS A FORM OF ABUSE
1. Appellant's Litigation Abuse Is Not Constitutionally Protected
 
There is no constitutional right to file abusive litigation. (See Wolfe v. George (2007) 486 F.3d 1120, 1125 [upholding California's vexatious litigant statute against a constitutional challenge].) California law recognizes that litigants can abuse the court process, as codified in the vexatious litigant statute. (Code Civ. Proc., � 391 et seq.) This statute permits a court to impose a bond or pre-filing requirements on litigants, and has repeatedly been upheld against constitutional challenge. (Wolfgram v. Wells Fargo Bank (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 43, 59 (hereafter Wolfgram); Childs v. PaineWebber Incorporated(1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 982, 992; In re RH(2009) 170 Cal.App.4th 678, 702 [not unconstitutional to apply vexatious litigant statute to a litigant in a dependency action].)
If it is constitutional for a court to impose pre-filing or bond restrictions on a litigant, then a judge's mere comment about a party's history of litigation clearly passes constitutional muster. Not only was the judge's observation of Mr. Bowen's obsessive litigation permissible, it was entirely appropriate. His litigation is part and parcel of his abuse.
Abusive ex-partners often employ �litigation abuse� or �paper abuse� as part of their tactics of harassment. (Miller & Smolter, Paper *22 Abuse: Documenting New Abuse Tactics (2012) vol. 17, No. 5, Domestic Violence Rep, 65, 76, attached at Exhibit 2; Note, Beyond the Bounds of Zealous Advocacy: The Prevalence of Abusive Litigation in Family Law and the Need for Tort Remedies (2007) 75 UMKC L. Rev. 1101,1110; National Council of Juvenile and Family Court Judges, Batterer Manipulation of the Courts to Further Their Abuse, and Remedies for Judges (2008) vol. 12, No. 1, Synergy 12.) Batterers often engage in paper abuse post-separation. (Miller, supra, vol. 17, No. 5, Domestic Violence Rep. at 65.) Their actions range from filing baseless lawsuits to making false allegations of child abuse, done �as a means of exerting power, forcing contact, and financially burdening their ex-partners.� (Ibid.)
Litigation abuse �shares some commonalities with �traditional� stalking, such as unwanted calls and letters... all of which harass, threaten, and intimidate the victim.� (Miller, supra, vol. 17, No. 5, Domestic Violence Rep. at 76 [citing generally Melton, Stalking in the Context of Intimate Partner Abuse (2007) 2 Feminist Criminology 347].) The difference between most paper abuse and �traditional� abuse is that using the court process is a lawful activity that �open[s] the door to further harassment under the guise of procedural equity.� (Id. at 75.)
The process of obtaining a restraining order can turn from an empowering act to a traumatic nightmare, with the stressors of repeated hearings, missed work, and encounters with abusers at court. (Miller, *23 supra, vol. 17, No. 5, Domestic Violence Rep. at 76.) Domestic violence survivors subject to paper abuse suffer �cumulative and debilitating effects� from this post-separation abuse. (Ibid.) Susan Miller's study of one group of survivors describes how just as they were trying to rebuild their lives, the women were crushed by litigation abuse:
...constantly thrust back into their former positions of being verbally challenged and attacked, causing them to relive situations from which they wanted to distance themselves and move forward with their lives. Being back in court answering to the offender's demands created a feeling of powerlessness in the women...
(Id. at 76.)
The California legislature recognized the prevalence of litigation abuse when it amended Family Code section 6345 to extend the renewal period of a restraining order from three years to five. (See Sen. Rules Com., Off. of Sen. Floor Analyses, 3d reading analysis of Assem. Bill No. 99 (2004-2005 Reg. Sess.) as amended March 1, 2005, p. 3.) The legislative history of the renewal statute acknowledges:
.while in three years the physical battering of the victim may have stopped, oftentimes the litigation is drawn out for many years and the court becomes the forum through which the batterers revictimize and traumatize the party protected by the order... (emphasis added)
(Ibid.)
 
Michel Bowen is a poster child for litigation abuse. The �four phone books� of documents in this case speak volumes (literally) about Appellant's obsessive, harassing behavior. Such a thick file is highly *24 unusual for a restraining order case without marriage, children, property, support, or any other complicating factor. Both judges to hear the case remarked upon Appellant's obsession. (RT2-5-09 at 10:18-11:13; RT 11-9-11 at 73:7-11.) Appellant has turned a relationship of just a few weeks into four years of litigation. He has received more �fair hearings� than most: two initial hearings, two hearings to reconsider, two appeals, a hearing on a motion to clarify, and a hearing on a motion to quash subpoena. (CT2 1-7.) In addition to his unceasing litigation, he has also falsely accused Ms. Lister of fraud at work. (RT 11-9-11 at 11:28-12:1.)
Ms. Lister described her fear of future abuse as stemming in part from Appellant's harassing litigation, and her words echo the experiences of the victims of paper abuse interviewed by Susan Miller:
.from the time that I first filed my order till now that his behavior is obsessed with me and this case and it's continuous filing of paperwork. It's continuous filing of taking me off of work and coming into court. It is continuous harassment, you know, for me to have to come here and fight to be protected...
(RT 11-9-11 at 10:9-14.)
As the Court of Appeal stated in Wolfgram v. Wells Fargo, �the right to petition has never been absolute.� (Wolfgram, supra, 53 Cal.App.4th at 56.) Appellant cites the litigation privilege to support his contention that the right to litigate is near absolute. The litigation privilege protects statements made in the course of litigation. (Civ. Code, � 47.) The litigation privilege does not create an unfettered right to litigate. No *25 litigation privilege, guarantee of counsel, or right to appeal shields a litigant from a judge's decision to take notice of reality.
The court' s reference to the number of lawyers retained by Appellant was not disparaging the use of lawyers but a recognition that when a litigant changes lawyers every few months,5 that usually indicates a problem with the litigant, not the lawyers.
2. Even If the Trial Court Erred In Considering Appellant's Excessive Litigation, the Error Was Not Prejudicial, As There Was Substantial Other Evidence to Support the Renewal of the Restraining Order
The California Constitution provides that the only errors that result in the reversal of a judgment are those that, after examination of the entire case, including all the evidence, result in �a miscarriage of justice.� (Cal. Const., art. VI, � 13; Soule v. General Motors Corp. (1994) 8 Cal.4th 548, 573 [judgment cannot be reversed unless error �caused actual prejudice in light of the whole record�].)
The Code of Civil Procedure further clarifies that an appellant must show that any error was �prejudicial,� and that they suffered �substantial injury� and �a different result would have been probable� absent the error. (Code Civ. Proc., � 475.) A different result is reasonably probable if there is a �reasonable chance,� and not just an �abstract possibility,� that the *26 result would have been different. (Cassim v. Allstate Insurance Co. (2004) 33 Cal.4th 780, 800 [quoting College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 715].)
The Evidence Code also precludes reversal for erroneously admitted evidence unless the error resulted in a �miscarriage of justice.� (Evid. Code, � 353.) Prejudice is not presumed from any error, and Appellant bears the burden of showing prejudicial error. (Code Civ. Proc., � 475; Hoffman St., LLC v. City of W. Hollywood (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 754, 772-73.)
In the present case, even if the trial court's reference to Mr. Bowen's history of litigation and his multiple lawyers was error, it was not prejudicial error. Erroneously considered evidence is harmless if the judgment is supported by other evidence. (9 Witkin, Cal. Procedure (5th ed. 2008) Appeal, � 430, p. 485; Hay v. Allen (1952) 112 Cal.App.2d 676, 681 [erroneous findings of fact will be ignored as harmless if judgment could be sustained on proper findings of the court].)
There was ample evidence to support the renewal of the restraining order, even if Appellant' s litigation abuse was ignored. The court considered Appellant's stalking which underlay the issuance of the first restraining order (RT 11-9-11 at 30:11-14, 71:18-22); his violation of the restraining order (id. at 71:3-4); his frequenting of the neighborhood where Ms. Lister lived (id. at 70:23-24); and his inappropriate involvement of her *27 family members in the litigation (id. at 70:23-26, 72:11-18). This evidence showed that Mr. Bowen continued to stalk Ms. Lister, and therefore that it was reasonable for her to fear future abuse. As much as his litigation abuse, these behaviors demonstrated that there had not been a change in circumstances to justify terminating the restraining order. There is no reasonable probability that the trial court would have failed to renew Ms. Lister's restraining order, even if the court ignored Mr. Bowen's history of litigation and his multiple attorneys.
The Court's discussion of Appellant's litigation history did not so impinge on his right to a fair hearing as to require reversal of the restraining order renewal. Appellant claims the court's reference to his litigation history denied him of his right to a fair hearing. (AOB 12). Automatic reversal is only required for �structural errors,� which are presumptively prejudicial because they affect �the framework within which the trial proceeds, rather than simply an error in the trial process itself.� (In re Angela C. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 389, 394 [quoting Arizona v. Fulminante (1991) 499 U.S. 279, 309-310].)
The kinds of errors which have been found to be structural are quite limited, and involve such fundamental errors as: deprivation of the right to counsel; bias by the trial court; race bias in the exclusion of grand jurors; denying self-representation or a public trial; and errors in reasonable-doubt jury instructions. (In re Angela C., supra, at 395 [citations omitted].)
*28 The trial court's reference to Mr. Bowen's numerous filings and attorneys did not implicate the fundamental fairness of the judicial proceedings. Even if it was error for the court to consider these issues, it was not a structural error which prevented Mr. Bowen from having a fair hearing.
C. THE INCLUSION OF MS. LISTER'S PLACE OF EMPLOYMENT IN THE RESTRAINING ORDER WAS NOT OVERBROAD AND DID NOT VIOLATE RESPONDENT'S ASSOCIATIONAL RIGHTS UNDER THE CONSTITUTION
Appellant resurrects his unsuccessful overbreadth argument from his first appeal and claims that the renewed restraining order was overbroad because, by including Ms. Lister's place of employment, it prevents him from �receiving or giving treatment to fellow alcoholics.� (AOB 15; Lister v. Bowen, supra, 2010 WL 2595137 at 9-10.)6 The Constitutions of the United States and California protect �a limited right of association,� not �a generalized right of �social association.� (People ex rel. Gallo v. Acuna (1997) 14 Cal.4th 1090, 1110 [quoting City of Dallas v. Stanglin (1989) 490 U.S. 19, 25] (hereafter Acuna).)
The First Amendment protects two kinds of associations: �those with an �intrinsic� or �intimate� value, and those that are �instrumental� to forms *29 of religious and political expression and activity.� (Ibid.) Mr. Bowen's association with the CATS organization is neither of an intimate nature or of a religious/political nature protected by the Constitution.
Intimate associations relate to marriage, raising children, and living with relatives. (Acuna, supra, 14 Cal.4th at 1110 [quoting Roberts v. U.S. Jaycees (1984) 468 U.S. 609, 619 (hereafter Roberts)].) Protected intimate associations must be �distinguished by such attributes as relative smallness, a high degree of selectivity in decisions to begin and maintain the affiliation, and seclusion from others in critical aspects of the relationship.� (Roberts, supra, 468 U.S. at 640.)
Appellant's relationship with the CATS organization is not such an intimate association. In Acuna, the California Supreme Court upheld an injunction prohibiting gang members from appearing together in a certain neighborhood. (Acuna, supra, 14 Cal.4th at 1110.) The court found that even though the gang members may have common values and enriching relationships, those relationships did not rise to the level of intimate or intrinsic associations. (Id. at 1112.) The U.S. Supreme Court has found that the associations among Rotary Club members do not warrant constitutional protection. Bd. of Directors of Rotary Int'l v. Rotary Club of Duarte (1987) 481 U.S. 537, 546-47. While Rotary Clubs benefit their members and provide valuable services to the community, they do not *30 constitute �the kind of private or personal relationship to which we have accorded protection under the First Amendment.� Id. at 547.
Like gang memberships or Rotary Clubs, the substance abuse counseling that Appellant gives or gets at CATS may be enriching to him or of benefit to the community, but it is not constitutionally protected. Furthermore, CATS is hardly selective or secluded in its affiliations. As Ms. Lister explained when discussing her job locations, CATS recruits many of its clients by outreaching to homeless substance abusers on the street. (RT 11-9-11 at 17:28-18:25, 39:15-17.)
The �instrumental� association protected by the First Amendment pertains to associations that involve an �individual's freedom to speak, to worship, and to petition the government for the redress of grievances.� (Acuna, supra, 14 Cal.4th at 1111 [quoting Roberts, supra, 468 U.S. at 622].) Those groups which are protected are generally formed �for the purpose of engaging in protected speech or religious activities.� (Ibid. [quoting Bd. of Directors of Rotary Internat. v. Rotary Club of Duarte supra, 481 U.S. at 544] [original italics].) CATS is not an organization �formed for the purpose of engaging in protected speech or religious activities.� Mr. Bowen has no constitutional right to associate with the staff or clients of Ms. Lister's employer.
Moreover, Appellant is not enjoined from associating with the �entire community of individuals� at CATS, as he claims in his brief. *31 (AOB 16.) The restraining order precludes Appellant from going within 100 yards of the four offices of the organization for which Ms. Lister works. (CT2 36, 40.) The order does not preclude Appellant from calling any member of the CATS organization, other than Ms. Lister. (Ibid.) Nor does the order preclude Appellant from socializing with or even working with members of the CATS organization as long as they are away from one of the four offices enumerated in the order and Ms. Lister is not present. (Ibid.) Such an order does not impinge on his constitutional rights to association. (See People v. Englebrecht (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 1236, 1263 [gang injunction that prevented certain family members from associating within a one square mile was not unconstitutional as they could see each other outside the target area].)
The protective order does not bar Appellant, as he claims, from �receiving or giving treatment to fellow alcoholics.� (AOB 15.) As noted in section III.A.4 above, dozens of sobriety programs are offered in the Bay Area and Appellant currently participates in many of them. (Bay Area Academy, �Bay Area Substance Abuse Resources� (July 21, 2012) \<http://bayareaacademy.org/downloads/
BayAreaSubstanceAbuseResources.pdf\>; Declaration of Michael Bowen at CT2 18:4-7.) Appellant presented no evidence that CATS was the only agency where he could receive or provide substance abuse counseling. Mr. Bowen was ordered to stay away from CATS *32 offices since September 10, 2008, and it appears he has managed to maintain his sobriety during the course of that time, and both receive and give treatment to fellow alcoholics at many other venues. (CT2 18:4-7.)
The restraining order was narrowly tailored to preserve Ms. Lister's safety by ordering Mr. Bowen to stay away from her place of work, in light of the fact that he stalked her in the parking lot that she used for work. (AR 4-6.) A court issuing a protective order under the Domestic Violence Prevention Act must issue the order on �forms adopted by the Judicial Council of California and that have been approved by the Department of Justice.� (Fam. Code, � 6221, subd. (c).) The Restraining Order After Hearing form, Judicial Council form DV-130, specifically includes a box that a court may check to order that a restrained party stay away from �the job or workplace� of a protected person. D. APPELLANT'S APPEAL IS UNTIMELY AS IT WAS FILED MORE THAN NINETY DAYS AFTER HE FILED HIS MOTION TO RECONSIDER
A party appealing an order that was upheld after a denial of a motion to reconsider under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a), must do so within the earliest of: (1) thirty days after the court clerk mails, or a party serves, an order denying the motion; (2) ninety days after filing the motion to reconsider; or (3) one-hundred eighty days after entry of judgment. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.108(b)(1)-(3).)
Appellant's appeal is untimely, as he filed his appeal more than ninety days after he filed his motion to reconsider. Appellant was served with the renewed Restraining Order After Hearing directly following the hearing on September 7, 2011. (Notice of Motion and Motion for Reconsideration at CT2 33:24-25.) He filed his motion to reconsider on *34 September 16, 2011. (CT2 29-34.) He filed his notice of appeal on December 22, 2011 (CT2 41-43), ninety-seven days after he filed his motion to reconsider. The trial court clearly denied Appellant's motion to reconsider. (RT 11-9-11 at 71:23-27.) The only change made by the trial court was to shorten the duration of the restraining order from five years to three years, a change the court had no authority to make. (Avalos, supra, 196 Cal.App.4th at 777 [trial court did not have discretion to renew a restraining order for only two years, as statute requires that renewal be for five years or permanently].) Appellant had requested the court to vacate the restraining order entirely, and the court did not do so. Thus his time to appeal runs from the filing of his motion to reconsider.
The revised Restraining Order After Hearing, filed December 14, 2011, did not restart Appellant's time to appeal. (Dakota Payphone, LLC v. Alcaraz (2011) 192 Cal.App.4th 493, 504-509.) The court in Dakota Payphone provided a thorough analysis of when amended judgments reset the time to appeal and concluded that an amended judgment only resets the clock when there is something in the amended order that could not have been challenged in the initial order. (Id. at 506-507.) In Dakota Payphone, the trial court initially entered a default judgment against Alcaraz. (Id. at 499.) Alcaraz filed a motion for a new trial, and the court granted that motion in part and reduced the damages owed by Alcaraz by about four million dollars. (Ibid.) Alcaraz then filed a notice of appeal from this *35 amended judgment and the court of appeal found that this appeal was untimely. (Id. at 499, 509.) The court found if Alcaraz wanted to challenge the court's initial judgment against him, he should have filed both a motion for a new trial and a notice of appeal, to preserve his right to appeal. (Id. at 509.) The court found that the reduction in damages did not reset the time to appeal. (Ibid.) It explained that while a reduction of four million dollars might seem to be significant, �[a]ll other parts of the judgment not affected by the modification remained valid and could have been challenged by appeal.� (Ibid.)
The revised restraining order filed December 14, 2011 changed only the expiration date of the order, and none of the personal restraint and stay away orders that Appellant is challenging in this appeal. Nor is Appellant challenging the length of the restraining order that was issued on December 14, 2011. Following the logic of Dakota Payphone, Appellant's time to appeal was not reset by this order. Instead his deadline was either sixty days after the first restraining order was served, or ninety days after his motion to reconsider was filed, whichever was later. He has missed both of these deadlines. Sixty days from September 7, 2011 was Sunday, November 6, 2011, so his deadline would have been November 7, 2011. Ninety days from September 16, 2011 was December 15, 2011.
The Court must dismiss Appellant's appeal as untimely. (Van Beurden Ins. Services, Inc. v. Customized Worldwide Weather Ins. Agency, *36 Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 51, 56 [time for appealing a judgment is jurisdictional and appellate court has no power to entertain a late-filed appeal].) Even when a notice of appeal is just one day late, the appellate court has no jurisdiction to hear the case, and must dismiss the appeal. (In re Hanley's Estate (1943) 23 Cal.2d 120, 121, 123.) California Rules of Court, rule 8.104(b) also is quite clear that a reviewing court must dismiss a notice of appeal that is filed late.
The Court could clearly dismiss this appeal without further comment. Given the important issues raised by Appellant's litigation abuse, Respondent respectfully requests that the Court address the issues briefed by Respondent in order to put to rest Appellant's harassment.
IV. CONCLUSION
Enough is enough.  Appellant's campaign of litigation abuse should be stopped now. The trial court properly renewed Respondent's domestic violence restraining order based on her reasonable fear of future abuse. This fear was justified by Appellant's violation of her restraining order and his obsessive litigation. Appellant has no constitutional right to harass Respondent through the courts or to receive treatment at her place of employment. Finally, Appellant's appeal is untimely, and should be dismissed.
