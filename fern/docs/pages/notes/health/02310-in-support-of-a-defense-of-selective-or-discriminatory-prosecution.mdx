In support of a defense of selective or discriminatory prosecution, liability lies in any express policy or an implicit custom adopted by the governmental unit. As per 42 U.S.C.A. � 1983, Patterson v. Burns, 670 F. Supp. 2d 837 (S.D. Ind. 2009), evidence of prosecutorial discrimination can be presented in two ways. Firstly, direct proof of a plan to discriminate can be presented, as seen in Murgia v. Municipal Court, 15 Cal. 3d 286, 124 Cal. Rptr. 204, 540 P.2d 44 (1975). Secondly, statistical data on prosecutorial conduct can be presented, showing that certain offenses are prosecuted only against a specific protected group, as exemplified in Bortin v. Superior Court, 64 Cal. App. 3d 873, 135 Cal. Rptr. 30 (1st Dist. 1976).

The key elements of a defense include that the defendant has been singled out for prosecution due to conduct forming the basis of the charges against them, and that the District Attorney�s discriminatory selection of defendants for prosecution was based on impermissible considerations, such as race, religion, or the desire to prevent the exercise of constitutional rights. These two essential elements are often referred to as �intentional and purposeful discrimination.� Once a defendant establishes a prima facie case, the burden shifts to the government to prove that the selection was based on non-discriminatory grounds.

Regarding the first prong of the Berrios test, which requires that the defendant has been intentionally singled out for prosecution, there is no doubt that the defendant is being prosecuted solely because of their financial status.
