Finally, consider the problem of the default judgment. The
history of most legal systems, including the common law, reflects a
period of intense struggle with the concept of default. Such conflict
seems almost invariably to have stemmed from a primitive conception
that no judgment could have validity without a bilateral hearing.
One escape, used in the early English common law and still earlier

description of the bilateral principle as one of equal opportunity to
be heard is truly a justification for giving judgment by default, or
whether it has merely become a symbol without content. We might
question, for example, whether the operation of our modern long-
arm statutes affords a really factual equality of opportunity to be
heard. We may wonder, too, about the extent to which such long-

facto ex parte. The principle of formal as opposed to rational proof. Formal
proof is that which attempts to objectify the value of matter presented
to the court and to relieve the court of the necessity of personal decision
about the truth or falsity of a particular allegation. The court must
give judgment, of course, but under a formal proof system the judg-
ment is dictated by the nature and quality of the evidence presented
rather than by the extent to which the fact-finder himself is persuaded.
An excellent example is the decisory oath, in which the statement of
one party, sworn according to specified forms, must be taken by the
court as conclusively establishing the truth of the fact stated. Rational
