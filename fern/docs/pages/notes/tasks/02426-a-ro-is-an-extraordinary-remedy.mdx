In order to prevail in a DVRO petition on a
claim of constitutional error, the complaining party must show
"actual prejudice." Hagler, 97 Wn.2d at 825-26. That is, the
petitioner must show that the error "so infected [the] entire trial
that the resulting conviction violates due process." In re Music,
104 Wn.2d 189, 191, 704 P.2d 144 (1985). To prevail in a
DVRO petition on a claim of non-constitutional error,
the complaining party must show the "error constitutes a
fundamental defect that inherently results in a complete
miscarriage of justice." Cook, 114 Wn.2d at 812. If a petitioner
fails to make a showing of prejudice, this Court must dismiss
the petition. In re Hews, 99 Wn.2d 80, 88, 660 P.2d 263 (1983).
Regardless of whether the petitioner bases his challenge
on constitutional or non-constitutional error, he must support
5his petition with facts or evidence supporting his claim of
unlawful restraint and not solely on conclusory allegations.
Cook, 114 Wn.2d at 813-14. Bare allegations unsupported by
citation to authority, references to the record, or persuasive
reasoning cannot sustain his burden of proof. State v. Brune, 45
Wn. App. 354, 363, 725 P.2d 454 (1986). Petitions including
nothing more than speculation, conjecture, or inadmissible
hearsay are equally insufficient. In re Gronquist, 138 Wn.2d
388, 396, 978 P.2d 1083 (1999) (citation omitted). As the
burden of proving actual prejudice rests with the petitioner,
'"[p]ossible prejudice will not be sufficient."' In re Ha verty,
101 Wn.2d 498, 506, 681 P.2d 835 (1984) ( quoting Hews, 99
Wn.2d at 93) ( emphasis in original).
