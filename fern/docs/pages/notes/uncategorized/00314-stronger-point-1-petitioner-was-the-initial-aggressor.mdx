STRONGER POINT 1: Petitioner Was the Initial Aggressor
The petitioner was the initial aggressor who provoked the conflict, and the respondent acted in reasonable self-defense. Courts must consider "the circumstances leading to the alleged abuse," including whether the respondent acted in reasonable self-defense. Valerie G. v. Louis G. (2017) 11 Cal. App. 5th 773, 783, 218 Cal. Rptr. 3d 200, 209. A person who responds reasonably to an aggressor does not commit abuse within the meaning of section 6203. Id.

STRONGER POINT 2: Acts Must Be Weighed Against Each Other
The petitioner's failure to acknowledge their own aggressive conduct renders their narrative legally inadequate because courts must weigh "the acts of the parties... against each other" rather than accepting one party's account in isolation. "Such a determination requires that the acts of the parties be weighed against each other. As a result, in deciding whether mutual restraining orders should issue, the trial court must consider the parties' respective alleged acts of domestic violence in concert, and not separately." K.L. v. R.H. (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 970, 979 (emphasis added).

STRONGER POINT 3: Most Significant Aggressor Standard
Where there is evidence that the petitioner was the "most significant aggressor," they are not entitled to protection. "Specifically, the statute mandates that the court determine which of the parties is the 'most significant' aggressor." K.L. v. R.H., supra, 70 Cal.App.5th at 979. The petitioner's complete omission of their own initiating conduct prevents the court from making this statutorily required determination.

STRONGER POINT 4: Refusal to Accept Responsibility as Evidence of Threat
The petitioner's refusal to accept responsibility for their own conduct�and their attempt to blame the respondent as the aggressor�demonstrates a propensity for future harm. In Putman v. Kennedy (Conn. App. 2007) 932 A.2d 434, 446, the court found that the father's "refusal to accept responsibility for that act, or to acknowledge that he should have handled the situation differently" and his blaming of the child "as the aggressor" was evidence he "posed a continuous threat."

STRONGER POINT 5: DVRO Used as Litigation Weapon
The petitioner is using the DVPA as a weapon to gain an unfair advantage in related proceedings rather than seeking genuine protection. "We are concerned... with the serious policy implications of permitting allegations of this nature to be branded as domestic violence and used by either spouse to secure rulings on critical issues such as support, exclusion from marital residence and property disposition, particularly when aware that a matrimonial action is pending or about to begin." J.N.S. v. D.B.S. (N.J. Super. 1997) 302 N.J. Super. 525, 530, 695 A.2d 730, 733 (reversing restraining order where petitioner failed to acknowledge own role). "Application of the domestic violence law to it trivialized the plight of true victims of domestic violence." Id.

STRONGER POINT 6: Mutual Combat - Both Parties Acted As Aggressors
Where the evidence shows both parties engaged in mutual combat, the petitioner cannot claim protected status without acknowledging their own role as an aggressor. "Section 6305 provides: 'The court may not issue a mutual order enjoining the parties from specific acts of abuse... unless... the court makes detailed findings of fact indicating that both parties acted primarily as aggressors and that neither party acted primarily in self-defense.'" Conness v. Satram (2004) 122 Cal. App. 4th 197, 206, 18 Cal. Rptr. 3d 577, 584 (emphasis added).

STRONGER POINT 7: History of Violence by Petitioner
The petitioner's failure to disclose their own history of violence toward the respondent renders their account incomplete and unreliable. "By separating out for analysis each party's claim of abuse against the other... a court does not give full effect to the statutory directive that it 'shall consider' both 'the history of domestic violence between the persons involved' and 'protect[ing] victims of domestic violence from continuing abuse.'" Melissa G. v. Raymond M. (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 360, 372, 238 Cal. Rptr. 3d 127, 140.

SYNTHESIZED CONCLUSION
The petitioner's unilateral, self-exonerating narrative is legally insufficient to support a restraining order. Where a petitioner fails to acknowledge their own role as the initial or dominant aggressor, offers no history of the relationship, and presents their account without the "hedges and qualifications" that would indicate credibility, they have failed to meet their burden. Courts must "weigh the acts of the parties... against each other" and determine the "most significant aggressor"�a determination impossible based on a one-sided, decontextualized account. K.L. v. R.H., supra, 70 Cal.App.5th at 979.
