RELEVANCE THEORY

Treating relevance as a property of propositions or assumptions, a common practice within the pragmatic literature, entails a considerable degree of abstraction. This analytical approach, while possessing undeniable utility, tends to overlook the inherently dynamic and interpretive nature of human cognition, as well as the active role an individual assumes in the construction of meaning. Within this intricate context, an individual litigant does not directly derive assumptions from mere utterances or, more broadly, from the myriad perceptible phenomena encountered within their environment. Instead, the raw sensory input generated by any given instance must first undergo a rigorous process of cognitive processing, filtering, and interpretation through a complex cognitive apparatus. Consequently, each discrete instance possesses the potential to generate a diverse array of plausible assumptions, the specific selection and subsequent salience of which are entirely contingent upon the litigant�s prevailing cognitive state. This encompasses, but is not limited to, their proximity to the other person, third party influences, current emotional disposition, and any inherent or acquired cognitive biases that may influence perception. Conversely, a phenomenon might be entirely disregarded as inconsequential if it fails to adequately engage these internal filters or align with the individual's current attentional focus. Therefore, for relevance theory to provide an adequate explanation of ostensive-inferential communication, particularly within complex and high-stakes domains such as legal proceedings, it is imperative that it precisely elucidate the mechanism by which attentional resources are directed toward a particular phenomenon. Furthermore, it must identify the specific assumption that is most likely to be constructed to describe that phenomenon. To achieve this comprehensive understanding, a revised definition is requisite, one that encompasses not merely the relevance of an abstract assumption, but more broadly, the relevance of the phenomenon itself as it is encountered, processed, and interpreted by an individual.
It is important to recognize that the selection or construction of an adequate context, achieved through the expansion of an initial contextual framework, necessitates a degree of cognitive effort. Similarly, the formulation of an assumption regarding a phenomenon, based upon the sensory stimulation it provides, also demands effort. Therefore, to transition our definition of the relevance of an assumption within a specific context to a comprehensive definition of the relevance of a phenomenon to an individual, it becomes necessary to augment the 'effort' component by including the cognitive expenditure required to both formulate an assumption and establish an appropriate context.
Relevance of a phenomenon to an individual, particularly in the context of abuse
When applied to the dynamics of abuse, the relevance of a phenomenon to an individual is determined by the intricate balance between the cognitive effects it yields and the processing effort required to achieve those effects. This framework is particularly valuable for understanding how individuals process complex, often ambiguous, communicative acts within these dynamics, differentiating between an individual's diverse and non-volitional communication styles and an abuser's deliberate coercive strategies. Specifically, it functions as follows:
Positive Cognitive Effects: In an abusive context, "positive cognitive effects" refer to cognitive outcomes that enhance an individual's understanding, safety, or capacity for self-preservation, even if the content of the phenomenon itself is distressing or harmful. These effects may include:
Clarification or Validation: Strengthening existing, perhaps nascent, assumptions about the abuser's patterns of behavior or validating one's own internal experiences and perceptions of the abuse.
Identification of Threat: Yielding new contextual implications that highlight deliberate coercive tactics, manipulative behaviors, or the escalating nature of the abuse, allowing for a clearer understanding of the abuser's intent.
Development of Coping Strategies: Facilitating the formulation of assumptions that contribute to developing protective measures, escape plans, or psychological resilience.
Recognition of Abuse: Enabling the individual to categorize a previously ambiguous or normalized interaction as abusive, thereby shifting their cognitive framework. The greater these adaptive cognitive effects achieved by processing the abusive phenomenon, the greater its relevance.
Processing Effort: This refers to the cognitive expenditure required to process the abusive phenomenon. In contexts of abuse, this effort can be significantly heightened due to psychological manipulation, trauma, and societal pressures. It encompasses:
Perceptual and Interpretive Effort: The effort required to perceive and acknowledge the abusive act or pattern, particularly when it is subtle, intermittent, or disguised. This includes the challenge of interpreting ambiguous signals or contradictory behaviors, and the significant effort to process information and communicate effectively when systematically silenced or dismissed, regardless of one's inherent communication style, as a means of navigating deliberate barriers.
Overcoming Cognitive Barriers: The effort to surmount internal obstacles such as denial, cognitive dissonance, gaslighting, self-blame, or ingrained loyalty that impede accurate processing.
Contextualization Effort: The effort involved in accessing or constructing an adequate context (e.g., recalling past similar incidents, integrating information from external sources, or applying therapeutic insights) to understand the phenomenon as abusive rather than isolated or deserved.
Emotional Regulation Effort: The cognitive resources expended to manage overwhelming emotional distress (e.g., fear, shame, confusion) that might otherwise prevent or distort the processing of the phenomenon. The smaller the effort required across these dimensions to process the abusive phenomenon, construct a relevant assumption from it, and access or construct an adequate context in which to process it, the greater its relevance.
In simpler terms, within the challenging landscape of abuse, a phenomenon is relevant to an individual to the extent that its processing yields significant, often survival-oriented, cognitive benefits for a minimal expenditure of the considerable effort involved in navigating such complex and distressing information. This lens is crucial for distinguishing between an individual's diverse, non-volitional communication styles and the intentional, coercive actions of an abuser.


PROXIMAL DISCOURSE 

While all cultures engage in verbal and nonverbal communication, the styles and interpretations of such communication exhibit substantial variation. The semantic value attributed to a particular communicative act or gesture may differ significantly across cultural contexts. Although this principle may appear self-evident, it is imperative to recognize that behaviors deemed acceptable in one cultural milieu (e.g., proximate conversational distance) may be perceived as intrusive or discourteous in another. Consequently, remote observation necessitates the avoidance of erroneous conclusions derived from stereotypical assumptions or insufficient empirical data. Instead, a culturally sensitive approach is paramount to understanding the potential interpretations of one's own conduct. As Einstein succinctly articulated, the capacity for observation is contingent upon the theoretical framework employed. A pivotal consideration is that the transformation of raw cues or data into verifiable information and evidence is not an inherently linear process. Such a transformation invariably necessitates the application of a guiding hypothesis, as raw cues and data do not intrinsically convey their meaning, relevance, or significance.
This inherent challenge extends to the reception and serious consideration of information, culminating in more profound issues when the credibility of the source is subject to scrutiny.
Miranda Fricker, a distinguished philosopher, posits that this phenomenon can manifest in a more extreme form, termed silencing or testimonial injustice. Testimonial injustice, by its very nature, precipitates fundamental communicative dysfunction. Specifically, a perceived deficit in credibility impedes the listener's capacity to adequately register or appropriately value the conveyed information, thereby invalidating the speaker's communicative intent. Thus, in a dyadic communicative exchange where individual (x) conveys discontent to individual (y) and individual (y) fails to respond, the latter effectively silences (x) through testimonial injustice by imputing an unwarranted credibility deficit. Drawing upon Fricker's framework, Langton�s example of refusal serves to elucidate this concept. Consider an individual who attributes a substantial credibility deficit to women, predicated on the perception that their decision-making is solely emotionally driven. Given this significant attribution of credibility deficit, the spoken words of a woman fail to register with his testimonial sensibilities. Despite her articulation of meaningful statements, the individual does not acknowledge her as an epistemic agent, thereby precluding her words from serving as a basis for his belief (140�1). This illustration underscores a crucial distinction from Langton's conceptualization of silencing. In Langton�s paradigm, the woman�s credibility remains unchallenged; her refusal is unsuccessful due to a failure of uptake, wherein the listener simply fails to comprehend the negative assertion. Conversely, in Fricker�s example, the man�s prejudicial identity-based bias leads him to actively discount the woman�s utterance as a substantive contribution to the discourse. Consequently, by failing to deem her words worthy of consideration, he effectively silences her refusal through the denial of her epistemic authority.
