Definitions

Vindictive Prosecution means a prosecution initiated in retaliation for the defendant exercising a protected constitutional or statutory right, as established in cases such as Blackledge v. Perry, 417 U.S. 21 (1974) and United States v. Goodwin, 457 U.S. 368 (1982).

Key factors demonstrating vindictive prosecution include the timing of increased charges after the defendant exercised legal rights, evidence that the prosecutor harbored genuine animus towards the defendant for exercising those rights, and the lack of an independent, legitimate basis for escalating the prosecution.

Selective Enforcement refers to the unconstitutional and discriminatory application of laws against certain individuals or groups, while similarly situated individuals are not prosecuted, in violation of the Equal Protection Clause as established in cases like Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886) and Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448 (1962).

To demonstrate selective enforcement, the defendant must show: (1) that they were singled out for prosecution while others similarly situated were not prosecuted; and (2) that the decision to prosecute was based on an unjustifiable standard such as race, religion, gender bias, retaliatory motive, or political pressure.

Due Process encompasses the constitutional rights and legal principles that aim to protect individuals from unfair, arbitrary, or abusive governmental actions, as guaranteed by the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments to the U.S. Constitution.

Key due process violations include punishing an individual for exercising a constitutional right, failing to provide adequate notice or a meaningful opportunity to be heard, and prosecutions motivated by vindictiveness or retaliation.
