Dear Editor,
Using Occam�s razor, the first place to
attack an argument is its assumptions, and
then if they are sound you must find fault
in the reasoning (or accept the argument).
The recent review of The Coddling of
the American Mind did neither. Rather,
the reviewer resorted to a technique
quite effective with the general public of
constructing a strawperson and then slaying
it. The readers of The Forum deserve mote.
The well-attributed premise of Coddling
is that our university graduates increasingly
lack key psycho-intellectual attributes
required for highly effective citizenship.
The authors systematically assign causation
_as the confluence of specifically identified
sociodemographic trends. Significantly, the
authors do not assign blame, and many times
state that folks causing the problems often
have the best of intentions. As with many
interesting human events, the causes of this
breakdown of effectiveness on campus are
diverse and mutually complicated. If an event
had a single simple cause, it most probably
would have happened before it did (e.g., the
Civil War) and would not be as interesting
to contemplate.
The authors self-identify as a political
moderate (that has never voted for a
Republican for president or Congress)
and a liberal. They bend over backwards
to admit that real prejudice exists on
campus and in society at large, but that is
not what they are addressing in this book
� strawperson #1. They talk about our
current hyperpartisan political environment
from their left-of-center perspective. The
reason that they do not talk about the
speech limitations of conservative colleges
is that progressive censorship of historically
left-wing ideas best illustrates the
nonpartisan dimension of their argument
� strawperson #2.
Despite the authors� admitted bias,
the book is consistent with a classically
conservative tenet that intentions by
themselves are irrelevant. Results are what
matter. True to this theme, the authors
offer specific remedies that can be taken by
parents, universities, and society at large.
This book is not about partisan politics or
prejudice; rather it suggests how we can
all help the next generation of American
citizens be confident critical thinkers
